# Evidence-based scientific writing patterns applied to slides

This reference abstracts generalizable judgments from a supplied mentor writing-style summary. Do not publish or copy its project examples into generated presentations.

1. One concrete throughline drives background, method, evidence and conclusion. A large 'integrated framework' name is not a substitute for a clear research problem.
2. Every important sentence should identify the **object**, **operation**, **output** and, when relevant, **purpose**. 'We introduce graph relations' is not enough: say which entities and how relations affect the prediction.
3. Name actual mechanisms and data instead of inflated abstractions. Distinguish technical terms such as alignment vs contrastive learning, structure vs semantics, and attention vs explanation.
4. Credit previous methods accurately: state what information they use and what they can accomplish before identifying a remaining, specific gap. Do not invent deficiencies for rhetorical contrast.
5. Associate each major method design with an earlier problem; associate each result or ablation with the design it actually tests. A module's presence alone is not evidence of usefulness.
6. Explain inputs, outputs and task definition before internal modules. Give mathematical variables plain-language referents.
7. For results, state a *supported* takeaway, then guide the audience to figure/table, metric and comparison, representative exact numbers and exceptions. Keep test protocol, sample size, metric direction and scope attached to the conclusion.
8. When mapping this to slides, a narrative step can span multiple pages. Do not force one section of a paper into exactly one slide or repeat the whole introduction on every page.
